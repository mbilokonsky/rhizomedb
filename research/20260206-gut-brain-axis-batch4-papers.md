## Executive Summary

This batch adds 10 verified papers spanning 2012-2020 to the knowledge graph, each filling a specific gap. The papers range from Claesson 2012's foundational elderly microbiome work in Nature to Ghosh 2020's Mediterranean diet intervention in Gut. The batch introduces Parkinson's disease (Scheperjans 2015), extends ASD coverage (Sharon 2019), adds the first Latin American institution-led study (Zurita 2020 from Ecuador), includes the first prebiotic-mood trial (Schmidt 2015), and provides the first multi-omics depression study (Zheng 2016). The psychobiotic meta-analysis slot is filled by Liu 2019, and the longitudinal population-scale cohort by Valles-Colomer 2019. Together these papers cover 7 countries, multiple study types, and significantly broaden the graph's taxonomic and metabolomic coverage.

All bibliographic details below have been cross-verified against PubMed, publisher pages, and/or DOI records. Where I could not independently confirm a specific detail (e.g., a precise bacterium mentioned in supplementary data), I note that explicitly.

The real challenge is not just finding 10 papers -- it is ensuring they form a *complementary* set that maximizes the knowledge graph's coverage along multiple axes simultaneously: geography, methodology, disease domain, life stage, and mechanistic pathway. Each paper was selected not only for its individual merit but for how it connects to the existing 27 papers and to the other 9 in this batch.

## Paper 1: Pediatric Gut-Brain

### Carlson 2018

**Full title:** Infant Gut Microbiome Associated With Cognitive Development

**First author surname + year:** Carlson 2018

**Journal:** Biological Psychiatry

**Volume/Pages:** Vol. 83, No. 2, pp. 148-159

**DOI:** 10.1016/j.biopsych.2017.06.021

**Study type:** cohort (cross-sectional with longitudinal follow-up at 1 and 2 years)

**Country/Institution:** USA / University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (Neuroscience Curriculum, Department of Psychiatry)

**Sample size:** 89 typically developing 1-year-old infants

**Key bacteria mentioned:**
- Bacteroides (dominant cluster associated with higher cognitive scores)
- Faecalibacterium
- Ruminococcaceae (cluster members)
- Three microbiota clusters identified via 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing (V1-V2 region)

**Key metabolites mentioned:**
- Not a metabolomics study; focused on microbial composition and brain imaging

**Key mechanisms mentioned:**
- Alpha diversity inversely associated with cognitive outcomes (counterintuitive finding)
- Microbiota cluster composition at age 1 predicts Mullen Scales scores at age 2
- Brain structural MRI showed minimal regional volume effects

**Key findings:**
Cluster analysis identified 3 groups of infants defined by bacterial composition at age 1. Mullen cognitive scores at age 2 differed significantly between clusters, with the Bacteroides-dominant cluster showing highest performance. Counterintuitively, higher alpha diversity at age 1 was associated with *lower* cognitive scores at age 2 -- suggesting that a focused, Bacteroides-rich community may be more developmentally beneficial than a diverse one in early life.

**Directionality:** Bacteroides -- associated with better neurodevelopmental outcomes; high alpha diversity -- associated with lower cognitive scores

**Source:** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28793975/) | [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0006322317317201)

## Paper 2: Prebiotic/Fiber Intervention

### Schmidt 2015

**Full title:** Prebiotic intake reduces the waking cortisol response and alters emotional bias in healthy volunteers

**First author surname + year:** Schmidt 2015

**Journal:** Psychopharmacology

**Volume/Pages:** Vol. 232, No. 10, pp. 1793-1801

**DOI:** 10.1007/s00213-014-3810-0

**Study type:** clinical_trial (randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled)

**Country/Institution:** UK / University of Oxford, Department of Psychiatry

**Sample size:** 45 healthy volunteers (3 groups of 15: FOS, B-GOS, placebo)

**Key bacteria mentioned:**
- Bifidobacterium spp. (known to be promoted by GOS; not directly measured in this study but mechanistically implicated)
- Study used prebiotics rather than direct microbial measurement

**Key metabolites mentioned:**
- Salivary cortisol (cortisol awakening response measured pre/post intervention)

**Key mechanisms mentioned:**
- HPA axis modulation: B-GOS reduced waking cortisol response
- Attentional bias modification: decreased vigilance to negative stimuli
- Prebiotic-driven Bifidobacterium proliferation as proposed mechanism
- Comparison between FOS and B-GOS (only B-GOS showed effects)

**Key findings:**
The salivary cortisol awakening response was significantly lower after 3 weeks of B-GOS (galactooligosaccharide) intake compared with placebo. Participants also showed decreased attentional vigilance to negative versus positive information in a dot-probe task after B-GOS. Critically, FOS (fructooligosaccharide) showed no effects, suggesting specificity of prebiotic type matters for neuroendocrine outcomes. This was one of the first studies demonstrating that a dietary prebiotic can alter stress hormone levels and emotional processing in humans.

**Directionality:** B-GOS intake -- decreased cortisol, decreased negative attentional bias

**Source:** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25449699/) | [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00213-014-3810-0)

## Paper 3: Longitudinal Cohort

### Valles-Colomer 2019

**Full title:** The neuroactive potential of the human gut microbiota in quality of life and depression

**First author surname + year:** Valles-Colomer 2019

**Journal:** Nature Microbiology

**Volume/Pages:** Vol. 4, pp. 623-632

**DOI:** 10.1038/s41564-018-0337-x

**Study type:** cohort (population-level cross-sectional with validation cohorts)

**Country/Institution:** Belgium / KU Leuven, Rega Institute for Medical Research (Raes Lab)

**Sample size:** 1,054 (Flemish Gut Flora Project primary cohort) + 1,070 in validation datasets

**Key bacteria mentioned:**
- Faecalibacterium prausnitzii (associated with higher quality of life)
- Coprococcus spp. (associated with higher quality of life; depleted in depression)
- Dialister spp. (depleted in depression)
- Butyricicoccus

**Key metabolites mentioned:**
- DOPAC (3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid) -- dopamine metabolite; microbial synthesis potential correlated with mental quality of life
- Butyrate (produced by Faecalibacterium, Coprococcus)
- GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) -- microbial production potential indicated role in depression

**Key mechanisms mentioned:**
- Gut-brain module analysis of faecal metagenomes
- Microbial neuroactive compound synthesis potential catalogued
- Butyrate production pathway as quality-of-life correlate
- DOPAC synthesis as mental health correlate
- GABA production potential linked to depression

**Key findings:**
In the largest population-level study of gut microbiome and mental health at the time, butyrate-producing Faecalibacterium and Coprococcus were consistently associated with higher quality of life indicators across cohorts. Coprococcus and Dialister were depleted in individuals with depression, even after correcting for confounding effects of antidepressants. The study pioneered the systematic cataloguing of microbial neuroactive compound synthesis potential in the human gut, linking DOPAC synthesis to mental well-being and GABA production to depression.

**Directionality:** Coprococcus, Dialister -- decreased_in_disease (depression); Faecalibacterium -- associated with higher quality of life

**Source:** [Nature Microbiology](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41564-018-0337-x) | [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30718848/)

## Paper 4: Latin America

### Zurita 2020

**Full title:** Analysis of gut microbiome, nutrition and immune status in autism spectrum disorder: a case-control study in Ecuador

**First author surname + year:** Zurita 2020

**Journal:** Gut Microbes

**Volume/Pages:** Vol. 11, No. 3, pp. 453-464

**DOI:** 10.1080/19490976.2019.1662260

**Study type:** case_control

**Country/Institution:** Ecuador / Universidad Tecnologica Equinoccial, Quito (Facultad de Ciencias de la Salud Eugenio Espejo, Centro de Investigacion Biomedica); collaboration with University of Illinois and University of Chicago

**Sample size:** 60 mestizo children (25 ASD cases, 35 matched controls)

**Key bacteria mentioned:**
- Bacteroides (increased in ASD)
- Ruminococcus (increased in ASD)
- Akkermansia (increased in ASD)
- Bifidobacterium (altered in ASD)
- Prevotella (altered in ASD)

**Key metabolites mentioned:**
- TGF-beta1 (transforming growth factor beta 1 -- elevated in serum of ASD children)
- Nutritional biomarkers (zinc, magnesium, vitamin D assessed)

**Key mechanisms mentioned:**
- Immune dysregulation (TGF-beta1 elevation)
- Nutritional deficiency as co-occurring factor
- Gut dysbiosis co-occurring with GI symptoms and abnormal food habits
- Environmental and dietary factors in a developing-country context

**Key findings:**
This is one of the few gut-brain axis studies from Latin America and the first from Ecuador. Children with ASD had higher prevalence of under- and overweight, more frequent GI symptoms, abnormal food habits, and elevated serum TGF-beta1. The gut microbiome composition differed between ASD and control groups, with increased Bacteroides and Ruminococcus in ASD. The study highlights how the gut-brain-immune triangle operates in a non-Western, under-studied population where nutritional and environmental confounders differ substantially from North American/European cohorts.

**Directionality:** Bacteroides, Ruminococcus, Akkermansia -- increased_in_disease (ASD); TGF-beta1 -- increased_in_disease (ASD)

**Note on Latin America representation:** The lead author (Maria Fernanda Zurita) is affiliated with Universidad Tecnologica Equinoccial in Quito, Ecuador. Senior collaborators are from US institutions. This reflects the common reality in Latin American microbiome research: local researchers leading studies with international collaboration due to sequencing infrastructure needs. A 2025 Nature Communications editorial noted that Latin American microbiome research remains "severely underrepresented" globally [Source: Nature Communications 2025].

**Source:** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31530087/) | [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7524316/)

## Paper 5: ASD Expansion

### Sharon 2019

**Full title:** Human Gut Microbiota from Autism Spectrum Disorder Promote Behavioral Symptoms in Mice

**First author surname + year:** Sharon 2019

**Journal:** Cell

**Volume/Pages:** Vol. 177, No. 6, pp. 1600-1618.e17

**DOI:** 10.1016/j.cell.2019.05.004

**Study type:** animal_study (germ-free mouse colonization with human donor microbiota)

**Country/Institution:** USA / California Institute of Technology (Caltech), Division of Biology and Biological Engineering (Mazmanian Lab)

**Sample size:** 16 human donors (8 ASD, 8 typically developing); colonized into germ-free mice

**Key bacteria mentioned:**
- Bacteroides ovatus (differentially abundant)
- Lactobacillus reuteri (differentially abundant)
- Donor-specific microbiota profiles transferred to mice
- ASD vs TD donor microbiota showed distinct community structures

**Key metabolites mentioned:**
- 5-aminovaleric acid (5AV) -- decreased in ASD-colonized mice; treatment with 5AV reduced ASD-like behaviors
- Taurine -- decreased in ASD-colonized mice; treatment with taurine reduced ASD-like behaviors
- GABA-related metabolites

**Key mechanisms mentioned:**
- Microbiota transplantation sufficient to induce behavioral changes (causal evidence)
- Alternative splicing of ASD-relevant genes in brains of ASD-colonized mice
- Metabolite-mediated modulation of neuronal excitability
- 5AV and taurine as GABA receptor agonists

**Key findings:**
Colonization of germ-free mice with gut microbiota from human ASD donors was sufficient to induce hallmark autistic behaviors (reduced sociability, increased repetitive behaviors), establishing causal evidence for microbiome involvement in ASD. Brains of ASD-colonized mice displayed alternative splicing of ASD-relevant genes. Metabolomic profiling identified 5-aminovaleric acid and taurine as depleted in ASD-colonized mice, and direct treatment with these metabolites improved behavioral abnormalities and modulated neuronal excitability. This paper moved the ASD-microbiome field from correlation to causation.

**Directionality:** 5-aminovaleric acid, taurine -- decreased_in_disease (ASD); Bacteroides ovatus -- differentially abundant in ASD

**Source:** [Cell](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/s0092-8674(19)30502-1) | [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31150625/) | [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6993574/)

## Paper 6: Psychobiotic Meta-Analysis

### Liu 2019

**Full title:** Prebiotics and probiotics for depression and anxiety: A systematic review and meta-analysis of controlled clinical trials

**First author surname + year:** Liu 2019

**Journal:** Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews

**Volume/Pages:** Vol. 102, pp. 13-23

**DOI:** 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2019.03.023

**Study type:** meta_analysis

**Country/Institution:** USA / Massachusetts General Hospital & Harvard Medical School (Department of Psychiatry)

**Sample size:** 34 controlled clinical trials included in the meta-analysis (thousands of participants across trials)

**Key bacteria mentioned:**
- Lactobacillus spp. (most commonly used probiotic genus across included trials)
- Bifidobacterium spp. (second most commonly used probiotic genus)
- Various prebiotic substrates targeting these genera

**Key metabolites mentioned:**
- Not a metabolomics study; focused on clinical outcomes (depression and anxiety symptom scales)

**Key mechanisms mentioned:**
- Probiotic modulation of mood via gut-brain axis
- Dose-response and strain-specificity as moderating factors
- Clinical vs. community sample as key moderator
- Distinction between prebiotic and probiotic efficacy

**Key findings:**
Across 34 controlled clinical trials, prebiotics did not differ significantly from placebo for depression or anxiety. Probiotics yielded small but significant effects for both depression (d = -0.24, p < .01) and anxiety (d = -0.10, p = .03). Critically, sample type was a moderator: effects were larger in clinical/medical samples (d = -0.45) and reached medium-to-large in psychiatric samples (d = -0.73). This suggests probiotics may be most effective precisely where they are most needed -- in clinically depressed populations -- but the evidence base from psychiatric samples remains small.

**Directionality:** Probiotics -- associated with reduced depression and anxiety symptoms (effect size moderated by clinical status)

**Source:** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31004628/) | [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6584030/)

## Paper 7: Parkinson's Gut-Brain

### Scheperjans 2015

**Full title:** Gut microbiota are related to Parkinson's disease and clinical phenotype

**First author surname + year:** Scheperjans 2015

**Journal:** Movement Disorders

**Volume/Pages:** Vol. 30, No. 3, pp. 350-358

**DOI:** 10.1002/mds.26069

**Study type:** case_control

**Country/Institution:** Finland / Helsinki University Central Hospital, Department of Neurology

**Sample size:** 144 (72 PD patients + 72 matched controls)

**Key bacteria mentioned:**
- Prevotellaceae (family) -- reduced by 77.6% in PD patients
- Enterobacteriaceae (family) -- positively associated with severity of postural instability and gait difficulty
- Classifier used four bacterial families for PD prediction

**Key metabolites mentioned:**
- Not a metabolomics study; focused on 16S rRNA-based microbiota composition (V1-V3 regions via pyrosequencing)

**Key mechanisms mentioned:**
- Prevotellaceae depletion as potential PD biomarker
- Enterobacteriaceae abundance correlating with motor phenotype severity
- Gut-brain axis involvement in neurodegenerative (not just psychiatric) disease
- Potential for microbiome-based diagnostic classifier

**Key findings:**
This was the first case-control study of the gut microbiome in Parkinson's disease. Prevotellaceae abundance was reduced by 77.6% in PD patients, with a threshold of 6.5% or less showing 86.1% sensitivity and 38.9% specificity. A logistic regression classifier combining four bacterial families and constipation severity achieved 66.7% sensitivity and 90.3% specificity for identifying PD patients. Enterobacteriaceae abundance correlated with motor symptom severity, suggesting the microbiome relates not just to disease presence but to clinical phenotype.

**Directionality:** Prevotellaceae -- decreased_in_disease (PD); Enterobacteriaceae -- increased_in_disease (PD, specifically postural instability/gait difficulty phenotype)

**Source:** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25476529/) | [Wiley](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/mds.26069)

## Paper 8: Diet Pattern Study

### Ghosh 2020

**Full title:** Mediterranean diet intervention alters the gut microbiome in older people reducing frailty and improving health status: the NU-AGE 1-year dietary intervention across five European countries

**First author surname + year:** Ghosh 2020

**Journal:** Gut

**Volume/Pages:** Vol. 69, No. 7, pp. 1218-1228

**DOI:** 10.1136/gutjnl-2019-319654

**Study type:** clinical_trial (multi-center dietary intervention, 1 year)

**Country/Institution:** Ireland / APC Microbiome Ireland, University College Cork; multi-center across UK, France, Netherlands, Italy, and Poland

**Sample size:** 612 non-frail or pre-frail elderly subjects across 5 European countries

**Key bacteria mentioned:**
- Faecalibacterium prausnitzii (enriched by MedDiet adherence)
- Roseburia (enriched by MedDiet adherence)
- Eubacterium (enriched by MedDiet adherence)
- Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron (enriched by MedDiet adherence)
- Ruminococcus torques (enriched in non-MedDiet group; associated with age-related disease)
- Collinsella aerofaciens (enriched in non-MedDiet group)
- Coprococcus comes (enriched in non-MedDiet group)
- Clostridium ramosum (enriched in non-MedDiet group)
- Veillonella dispar (enriched in non-MedDiet group)

**Key metabolites mentioned:**
- Short-chain fatty acids / branched-chain fatty acids (increased with MedDiet)
- Secondary bile acids (decreased with MedDiet)
- p-Cresols (decreased with MedDiet)
- C-reactive protein (CRP) -- inflammatory marker, negatively associated with MedDiet-enriched taxa
- Interleukin-17 (IL-17) -- negatively associated with MedDiet-enriched taxa

**Key mechanisms mentioned:**
- Diet-driven microbiome modulation over 1 year
- SCFA production as anti-inflammatory mediator
- Reduction in secondary bile acids and p-cresols (markers of proteolytic fermentation)
- Cross-country consistency of microbiome-diet response despite baseline differences
- Microbiome as mediator between diet and frailty/cognitive outcomes

**Key findings:**
A 1-year Mediterranean diet intervention across 612 elderly subjects in 5 European countries led to consistent microbiome changes regardless of country. MedDiet adherence enriched anti-inflammatory, SCFA-producing bacteria (Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Eubacterium) and reduced bacteria associated with age-related disease (Ruminococcus torques, Collinsella). These microbiome changes correlated with lower frailty scores, improved cognitive function, and reduced inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-17). This is arguably the most comprehensive diet-microbiome-health intervention study in the elderly to date.

**Directionality:** F. prausnitzii, Roseburia, Eubacterium -- increased with MedDiet (beneficial); R. torques, Collinsella, C. ramosum -- decreased with MedDiet (deleterious when elevated)

**Source:** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32066625/) | [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7306987/)

## Paper 9: Multi-Omics

### Zheng 2016

**Full title:** Gut microbiome remodeling induces depressive-like behaviors through a pathway mediated by the host's metabolism

**First author surname + year:** Zheng 2016

**Journal:** Molecular Psychiatry

**Volume/Pages:** Vol. 21, pp. 786-796

**DOI:** 10.1038/mp.2016.44

**Study type:** case_control (with animal model validation via FMT into germ-free mice)

**Country/Institution:** China / Chongqing Medical University, Institute of Neuroscience; Department of Neurology, First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University

**Sample size:** 121 humans (58 MDD patients + 63 healthy controls); germ-free mouse models for validation

**Key bacteria mentioned:**
- Firmicutes (altered abundance in MDD)
- Actinobacteria (altered abundance in MDD)
- Bacteroidetes (altered abundance in MDD)
- Specific species-level data in supplementary materials

**Key metabolites mentioned:**
- Hippuric acid (identified as depression-associated urinary metabolite in related work by same group)
- Amino acid metabolites (disturbed in mice with "depression microbiota")
- Carbohydrate metabolites (disturbed in mice with "depression microbiota")
- The study used both GC-MS and LC-MS metabolomics alongside 16S rRNA sequencing

**Key mechanisms mentioned:**
- Fecal microbiota transplantation from MDD patients to germ-free mice induced depressive-like behaviors (causal evidence)
- Host metabolism as mediating pathway between microbiome and behavior
- Carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism disturbances as key pathway
- Bidirectional microbiome-metabolism interaction

**Key findings:**
This landmark multi-omics study combined metagenomic sequencing with metabolomic profiling in both human MDD patients and germ-free mouse models. MDD patients showed significantly different gut microbiota composition (altered Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes ratios) compared to controls. Crucially, transplanting "depression microbiota" from MDD patients into germ-free mice induced depressive-like behaviors, with the mice exhibiting disturbances in microbial genes and host metabolites involved in carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism. This was one of the first studies to demonstrate causal microbiome-to-depression pathways through combined omics approaches.

**Directionality:** "Depression microbiota" -- causally linked to depressive-like behaviors via metabolic disruption

**Source:** [Nature/Molecular Psychiatry](https://www.nature.com/articles/mp201644) | [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27067014/)

## Paper 10: Aging/Elderly

### Claesson 2012

**Full title:** Gut microbiota composition correlates with diet and health in the elderly

**First author surname + year:** Claesson 2012

**Journal:** Nature

**Volume/Pages:** Vol. 488, No. 7410, pp. 178-184

**DOI:** 10.1038/nature11319

**Study type:** cohort (cross-sectional with residential-setting stratification)

**Country/Institution:** Ireland / University College Cork, Department of Microbiology; Teagasc Food Research Centre, Moorepark, Fermoy, Co. Cork

**Sample size:** 178 elderly subjects (stratified by community-dwelling, day-hospital, rehabilitation, long-term residential care)

**Key bacteria mentioned:**
- Bacteroides (enriched in animal-product-heavy diets)
- Alistipes (enriched in animal-product-heavy diets)
- Prevotella (enriched in plant-based/healthier diets)
- Ruminococcus (decreased in long-stay care)
- Oscillibacter (increased in long-stay care)
- Parabacteroides
- Faecalibacterium

**Key metabolites mentioned:**
- Short-chain fatty acids (measured in faecal water; correlated with microbiota and health)
- Faecal water metabolites profiled broadly

**Key mechanisms mentioned:**
- Diet as primary driver of microbiota variation in the elderly
- Residence location (community vs. long-term care) as proxy for diet and lifestyle
- Loss of microbiota diversity in institutional settings correlating with increased frailty
- Inflammatory markers correlating with microbiota composition
- Co-morbidity and nutritional status as microbiome correlates

**Key findings:**
In 178 elderly Irish subjects, faecal microbiota composition correlated strongly with residence location (community-dwelling vs. long-term care), which itself tracked diet. Long-stay residents had significantly less diverse microbiota than community dwellers, and this loss of diversity correlated with increased frailty, co-morbidity, and inflammatory markers. Diet composition (animal-product vs. plant-based) drove microbiota differences (Bacteroides/Alistipes vs. Prevotella dominance). This foundational study established the diet-microbiome-health triangle in aging and demonstrated that institutional living environments fundamentally reshape the gut ecosystem.

**Directionality:** Microbiota diversity -- decreased in frail/institutionalized elderly; Prevotella -- associated with healthier outcomes; Bacteroides/Alistipes -- associated with animal-product-heavy institutional diets

**Note:** While this paper predates the 2015-2024 preferred range, it is included because it is THE foundational study of elderly gut microbiome and health, published in Nature with enormous citation impact, and no subsequent paper has superseded it as the reference point for this domain.

**Source:** [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature11319) | [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22797518/)

## Invariant Findings Across All 10 Papers

1. **Butyrate-producing bacteria are consistently beneficial.** Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Coprococcus, and Roseburia appear repeatedly as health-associated taxa across pediatric (Carlson), depression (Valles-Colomer), diet (Ghosh), and aging (Claesson) contexts.

2. **The microbiome can causally influence behavior.** Both Zheng 2016 (depression) and Sharon 2019 (ASD) used fecal microbiota transplantation into germ-free mice to demonstrate that microbiome composition is sufficient to induce behavioral changes.

3. **Diet is the most powerful modifiable driver of microbiome composition.** Claesson 2012, Ghosh 2020, and Schmidt 2015 all demonstrate this from different angles (institutional diet, Mediterranean intervention, prebiotic supplementation).

4. **Effect sizes for probiotic interventions are modest but real.** Liu 2019's meta-analysis found small-to-medium effects, larger in clinical populations. This is consistent with the complexity of the gut-brain axis.

## Points of Divergence

1. **Alpha diversity paradox.** Carlson 2018 found higher infant gut diversity associated with *lower* cognitive scores, while Claesson 2012 found diversity loss in the elderly associated with *worse* health. This suggests the diversity-health relationship is life-stage dependent.

2. **Prebiotic specificity.** Schmidt 2015 found B-GOS effective but FOS ineffective for cortisol/mood. This creates tension with meta-analyses that group all prebiotics together.

3. **Geographic confounders.** Zurita 2020 highlights how nutritional status, dietary patterns, and environmental exposures in Ecuador create a fundamentally different context for interpreting microbiome-ASD associations compared to US/European studies.

4. **Psychiatric vs. neurodegenerative framing.** Scheperjans 2015 (Parkinson's) involves overlapping mechanisms (inflammation, SCFA, vagus nerve) with psychiatric studies but is studied by entirely different research communities with different methodological traditions.

## Rabbit Holes Explored

- **The Bravo 2011 PNAS paper** (Lactobacillus rhamnosus, vagus nerve, GABA -- from Cryan's lab in Cork) kept appearing as a foundational citation across multiple search paths. It is already well-known but its influence on subsequent work (Schmidt 2015, Valles-Colomer 2019) is worth noting as a connective node.

- **Latin American microbiome research scarcity** is a documented structural problem. A 2025 Nature Communications editorial called it "severely underrepresented." The Zurita 2020 paper exists partly because US institutions provided sequencing infrastructure. This gap is not about lack of scientific interest but about research funding geography.

- **Nikolova 2021 JAMA Psychiatry** was a strong alternative for the psychobiotic/meta-analysis slot. It is a transdiagnostic meta-analysis of 59 case-control studies finding depleted butyrate-producers across depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and anxiety. I chose Liu 2019 because it specifically meta-analyzes *interventions* (probiotics/prebiotics) rather than observational microbiome composition, which better fills the "psychobiotic meta-analysis" gap.

## Edge Cases and Gotchas

1. **Carlson 2018 volume/issue note:** Published online August 2017, but assigned to Biological Psychiatry Volume 83, Issue 2 (January 15, 2018). Citation databases may list either year.

2. **Zurita 2020 publication date:** Epub September 18, 2019; print publication in Gut Microbes Volume 11, Issue 3, 2020. Some databases list this as Zurita 2019.

3. **Zheng 2016 metabolomics scope:** The paper uses GC-MS and LC-MS metabolomics alongside 16S sequencing, qualifying as multi-omics. However, it uses 16S rRNA sequencing rather than shotgun metagenomics. Strictly speaking, "metagenomics" usually implies shotgun sequencing. I included it because it pioneered the combined omics approach for depression-microbiome research and the FMT validation adds a mechanistic layer that pure correlational multi-omics studies lack.

4. **Claesson 2012 is pre-2015.** At 2012, it falls outside the preferred 2015-2024 window, but no subsequent aging-microbiome study has achieved comparable impact. The next closest candidate would be the Ghosh 2020 paper (already included for diet). An alternative 2015+ aging paper would be less impactful.

## Practical Implications for the Knowledge Graph

These 10 papers add:
- **2 new disease domains:** Parkinson's disease (Scheperjans), cognitive decline/frailty in aging (Claesson, Ghosh)
- **1 new life stage:** Infancy/pediatric (Carlson)
- **1 new geography:** Latin America/Ecuador (Zurita)
- **3 new study types not previously covered:** prebiotic intervention (Schmidt), multi-omics (Zheng), diet pattern intervention (Ghosh)
- **Extensive new bacteria:** Prevotellaceae (PD-specific), Oscillibacter, Alistipes, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, Collinsella aerofaciens, Ruminococcus torques
- **Key new metabolites:** DOPAC, 5-aminovaleric acid, taurine, p-cresols, secondary bile acids, cortisol (as outcome)
- **Causal evidence papers:** Sharon 2019 and Zheng 2016 both establish causation via FMT into germ-free mice

## Open Questions

1. **Should Nikolova 2021 (JAMA Psychiatry) be added as an 11th paper?** It fills a slightly different niche than Liu 2019 -- transdiagnostic observational meta-analysis vs. intervention meta-analysis. Both are valuable.

2. **Is there a better Latin American paper?** The Zurita 2020 paper is genuinely Ecuador-led but co-authored with US institutions. A 2024 Brazilian birth cohort study exists (Carvalho et al., Scientific Reports, DOI: 10.1038/s41598-024-81774-4) but is very recent and lower-impact.

3. **Should Sampson 2016 (Cell) replace Scheperjans 2015 for the Parkinson's slot?** Sampson et al. showed gut microbiota regulate motor deficits and neuroinflammation in a PD mouse model. It is higher-impact but is an animal study. Scheperjans 2015 was chosen for being the first human PD-microbiome study.

4. **Multi-omics definition ambiguity:** Does 16S + metabolomics count as "multi-omics"? Purists would say shotgun metagenomics + metabolomics. If the graph needs strict metagenomics, consider Zheng's later work or the Hu 2022 bipolar disorder multi-omics study (Molecular Psychiatry, DOI: 10.1038/s41380-022-01569-9) as an alternative.
