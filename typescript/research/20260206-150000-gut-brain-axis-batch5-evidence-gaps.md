## Executive Summary

This batch of 10 papers was selected to address six identified evidence gaps in the existing 35-paper knowledge graph: (1) Parkinson's disease representation beyond the single Scheperjans 2015 paper, (2) complete absence of ADHD-microbiome research, (3) geographic skew toward Western populations, (4) overreliance on cross-sectional designs, (5) need for specific probiotic strain trials with named outcomes, and (6) diet intervention RCTs measuring both microbiome and mental health.

The selections include 2 Parkinson's papers (Sampson 2016 -- arguably the most important PD-microbiome paper ever published -- and Keshavarzian 2015), 2 ADHD papers (Aarts 2017 and Tengeler 2020), 1 multi-ethnic cohort including African and Asian-descent populations (Bos 2022), 1 large-scale population-based association study with replication cohort (Radjabzadeh 2022), 1 specific probiotic strain RCT with fMRI outcome (Pinto-Sanchez 2017), 1 diet intervention RCT (Jacka 2017), 1 fecal transplant study from China (Zheng 2016 -- note: this is a *different* Zheng 2016 from what might be in the graph; see discussion), and 1 Parkinson's SCFA-mechanism study from Germany (Unger 2016).

Several papers serve double duty across gaps. The Bos 2022 HELIUS study addresses both the geographic diversity gap (Ghanaian, Turkish, Moroccan, South-Asian Surinamese, African Surinamese populations) and the large-sample population-based design gap. Sampson 2016 addresses both PD representation and the fecal transplant/causal mechanism gap.

## The Question Beneath the Question

The surface request is "find 10 more papers." But the real question is: what are the structural weaknesses in our evidence base that could lead to incorrect generalizations? The existing graph is strong on anxiety/depression in Western populations using cross-sectional designs, but it has critical blind spots:

- **Neurodegeneration is underrepresented.** Parkinson's disease is arguably where the gut-brain axis evidence is *most* compelling for causal directionality (prodromal GI symptoms precede motor symptoms by years/decades), yet we have only one PD paper.
- **Neurodevelopmental conditions are absent.** ADHD affects ~5% of children and ~2.5% of adults worldwide. The microbiome-ADHD connection, while newer, involves dopamine pathway modulation -- a mechanism distinct from the serotonin/GABA pathways dominant in our depression papers.
- **Geographic homogeneity creates false universals.** If all our microbiome-depression associations come from Western populations eating Western diets, we cannot know which associations are biological invariants vs. diet-dependent artifacts.
- **Cross-sectional designs cannot establish temporality.** We need at least some prospective/longitudinal evidence to distinguish "microbiome changes cause mood changes" from "mood changes cause microbiome changes" from "shared upstream cause."

### Paper 1: Sampson et al. 2016 -- The Landmark PD-Microbiome Causal Evidence

**Full citation:** Sampson TR, Debelius JW, Thron T, Janssen S, Shastri GG, Ilhan ZE, Challis C, Schretter CE, Rocha S, Gradinaru V, Chesselet MF, Keshavarzian A, Shannon KM, Krajmalnik-Brown R, Wittung-Stafshede P, Knight R, Mazmanian SK. (2016). Gut microbiota regulate motor deficits and neuroinflammation in a model of Parkinson's disease. *Cell*, 167(6), 1469-1480.e12.

**DOI:** 10.1016/j.cell.2016.11.018

**Study type:** Animal study (germ-free mice + fecal transplant from human PD patients)

**Sample size:** Mouse model study using alpha-synuclein-overexpressing (ASO) mice. Multiple experimental arms with n=4-12 per group across different experiments. Human fecal donors: PD patients vs. healthy controls (exact n for donors not specified in available abstracts; compiled from 2 independent cohorts).

**Country/geography:** USA (California Institute of Technology, Pasadena)

**Key findings:**
- Germ-free ASO mice showed reduced motor deficits, microglia activation, and alpha-synuclein aggregation compared to specific-pathogen-free ASO mice
- Antibiotic treatment in young ASO mice ameliorated PD pathology
- Colonization of germ-free ASO mice with microbiota from PD patients enhanced motor dysfunction significantly more than microbiota from healthy human donors
- Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) alone were sufficient to promote neuroinflammation and motor deficits in germ-free ASO mice
- Establishes a causal pathway: gut microbiota -> SCFAs -> microglia activation -> alpha-synuclein aggregation -> motor deficits

**Bacteria/mechanisms involved:** Complex microbiota rather than individual species; SCFAs as key mediators; microglia activation as downstream mechanism; alpha-synuclein aggregation promoted by gut-derived signals.

**Why it fills an evidence gap:** This is arguably the single most important paper in the PD-microbiome field. It moves beyond correlational evidence (which Scheperjans 2015 provided) to demonstrate *causality* using germ-free mouse models and human fecal transplantation. Published in *Cell*, it is one of the most highly cited papers in the entire gut-brain axis literature. It fills the Parkinson's gap (#1) and adds the first fecal transplant evidence to our graph for a neurodegenerative condition.

**Source:** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27912057/) | [Cell Full Text](https://www.cell.com/fulltext/S0092-8674(16)31590-2)

### Paper 2: Keshavarzian et al. 2015 -- Colonic Bacterial Composition in Parkinson's Disease

**Full citation:** Keshavarzian A, Green SJ, Engen PA, Voigt RM, Naqib A, Forsyth CB, Mutlu E, Shannon KM. (2015). Colonic bacterial composition in Parkinson's disease. *Movement Disorders*, 30(10), 1351-1360.

**DOI:** 10.1002/mds.26307

**Study type:** Case-control (mucosal biopsies + fecal samples)

**Sample size:** 38 PD patients, 34 healthy controls (66 sigmoid mucosal biopsies, 65 fecal samples)

**Country/geography:** USA (Rush University Medical Center, Chicago)

**Key findings:**
- Both mucosal and fecal microbial communities of PD patients significantly differed from controls, with fecal differences more pronounced
- Butyrate-producing bacteria (anti-inflammatory) were significantly more abundant in healthy controls than PD patients
- Putative proinflammatory Proteobacteria (including Enterobacteriaceae) were more abundant in PD fecal samples
- At the genomic level, genes for LPS biosynthesis and type III bacterial secretion systems were increased in PD
- Genes associated with metabolism were significantly reduced in PD fecal microbiome
- Supports the hypothesis that proinflammatory dysbiosis may trigger alpha-synuclein misfolding

**Bacteria involved:** Decreased: butyrate-producing bacteria (Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Blautia). Increased: Enterobacteriaceae, Proteobacteria. Direction: proinflammatory shift in PD.

**Why it fills an evidence gap:** Complements Scheperjans 2015 (already in graph) by providing both mucosal AND fecal microbiome data -- critically, this is one of the few PD studies to sample the sigmoid mucosa directly rather than relying solely on stool. The mucosal vs. fecal comparison adds methodological depth. Also provides genomic functional pathway analysis (LPS biosynthesis, type III secretion), connecting taxonomic changes to mechanistic implications. Fills PD gap (#1).

**Source:** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26179554/) | [Movement Disorders](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/mds.26307)

### Paper 3: Unger et al. 2016 -- Short Chain Fatty Acids in Parkinson's Disease

**Full citation:** Unger MM, Spiegel J, Dillmann KU, Grundmann D, Philippeit H, Burmann J, Fassbender K, Schwiertz A, Schafer KH. (2016). Short chain fatty acids and gut microbiota differ between patients with Parkinson's disease and age-matched controls. *Parkinsonism & Related Disorders*, 32, 66-72.

**DOI:** 10.1016/j.parkreldis.2016.08.019

**Study type:** Case-control

**Sample size:** 34 PD patients, 34 age-matched controls

**Country/geography:** Germany (Saarland University, Homburg)

**Key findings:**
- Fecal SCFA concentrations (acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid) were significantly reduced in PD patients compared to controls
- First study to directly measure fecal SCFA concentrations in PD patients
- Bacteroidetes and Prevotellaceae were reduced in PD fecal samples
- Enterobacteriaceae were more abundant in PD
- Reduced SCFAs may contribute to enteric nervous system alterations and gastrointestinal dysmotility in PD
- Confirms the SCFA-depletion mechanism that Sampson 2016 demonstrated experimentally

**Bacteria/mechanisms involved:** Decreased: Bacteroidetes, Prevotellaceae (SCFA producers). Increased: Enterobacteriaceae. Mechanism: SCFA depletion -> ENS alterations -> GI dysmotility. Direction: reduced anti-inflammatory metabolites in PD.

**Why it fills an evidence gap:** This is the mechanistic bridge paper for Parkinson's. While Sampson 2016 showed SCFAs are sufficient to cause PD pathology in mice, and Keshavarzian 2015 showed butyrate-producing bacteria are depleted in PD, Unger 2016 directly measured the SCFA metabolites themselves in human PD patients and found them reduced. Together, these three papers form a complete chain: depleted SCFA-producing bacteria -> reduced fecal SCFAs -> PD pathology. Fills PD gap (#1) and adds European/German geographic representation.

**Source:** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27591074/) | [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1353802016303236)

### Paper 4: Aarts et al. 2017 -- Gut Microbiome in ADHD and Neural Reward

**Full citation:** Aarts E, Ederveen THA, Naaijen J, Zwiers MP, Boekhorst J, Timmerman HM, Smeekens SP, Netea MG, Buitelaar JK, Franke B, van Hijum SAFT, Arias Vasquez A. (2017). Gut microbiome in ADHD and its relation to neural reward anticipation. *PLoS ONE*, 12(9), e0183509.

**DOI:** 10.1371/journal.pone.0183509

**Study type:** Case-control with fMRI neuroimaging

**Sample size:** 19 ADHD participants, 77 controls. fMRI subset: 28 participants.

**Country/geography:** Netherlands (Radboud University Medical Center, Nijmegen)

**Key findings:**
- Nominal increase in Bifidobacterium genus abundance in ADHD cases
- Significantly enhanced predicted gene function for cyclohexadienyl dehydratase (enzyme in phenylalanine synthesis, a dopamine precursor) in ADHD vs. controls
- This increased dopamine-precursor synthesis function was associated with *decreased* ventral striatal fMRI responses during reward anticipation, independent of ADHD diagnosis and age
- First study to characterize the gut microbiome in ADHD using next-generation 16S rRNA sequencing
- Connects microbiome composition to brain function via dopamine pathway

**Bacteria/mechanisms involved:** Bifidobacterium (increased in ADHD). Mechanism: bacterial phenylalanine synthesis -> dopamine precursor availability -> altered reward processing in ventral striatum. Direction: microbiome-level changes in dopamine pathway linked to reduced neural reward anticipation.

**Why it fills an evidence gap:** This is the foundational ADHD-microbiome paper. It fills the complete ADHD absence (#2) in our graph. Critically, it provides a mechanistic link (dopamine pathway) that is distinct from the serotonin/GABA pathways dominating our depression papers, and it combines microbiome data with fMRI neuroimaging -- a multimodal approach missing from our graph. The sample size is small (n=19 ADHD), which is an important limitation to note.

**Source:** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28863139/) | [PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0183509)

### Paper 5: Tengeler et al. 2020 -- ADHD Microbiota Transplant into Mice

**Full citation:** Tengeler AC, Dam SA, Wiesmann M, Naaijen J, van Bodegom M, Belzer C, Debelius JW, Zilber N,"; Buitelaar JK, Kozicz T, Arias Vasquez A, Kiliaan AJ. (2020). Gut microbiota from persons with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder affects the brain in mice. *Microbiome*, 8, 44.

**DOI:** 10.1186/s40168-020-00816-x

**Study type:** Fecal transplant (human -> germ-free mice) + MRI neuroimaging

**Sample size:** Fecal donors: 3 ADHD patients, 3 healthy controls (pooled). Recipients: germ-free C57BL/6JOlaHsd mice, approximately 6-8 per group.

Mechanism: microbiome composition -> altered brain structural integrity -> altered functional connectivity -> behavioral changes. Direction: ADHD microbiota causally impairs brain white/gray matter and increases anxiety.

**Why it fills an evidence gap:** Builds directly on Aarts 2017 by moving from correlational to causal evidence for the ADHD-microbiome connection. The use of fecal transplant + neuroimaging in the recipients is a powerful design. Together with Aarts 2017, these two papers bracket the ADHD evidence: one shows the human association + mechanism (dopamine), the other demonstrates causality via FMT. Fills ADHD gap (#2) with the strongest available causal evidence.

### Paper 6: Bosch (Bos) et al. 2022 -- Gut Microbiota and Depression Across Ethnic Groups (HELIUS Cohort)

**Full citation:** Bosch JA, Nieuwdorp M, Zwinderman AH, Deschasaux M, Radjabzadeh D, Kraaij R, Davids M, de Rooij SR, Lok A. (2022). The gut microbiota and depressive symptoms across ethnic groups. *Nature Communications*, 13, 7129.

**DOI:** 10.1038/s41467-022-34504-1

**Study type:** Cross-sectional population-based cohort (multi-ethnic)

**Sample size:** N = 3,211 across 6 ethnic groups: Dutch, South-Asian Surinamese, African Surinamese, Ghanaian, Turkish, Moroccan -- all living in Amsterdam.

**Country/geography:** Netherlands (Amsterdam), but critically includes populations of African (Ghanaian, African Surinamese), South Asian (South-Asian Surinamese), Middle Eastern (Turkish), and North African (Moroccan) descent.

**Key findings:**
- Both alpha-diversity (within-individual) and beta-diversity (between-individual) predicted depressive symptom levels after controlling for demographic, behavioral, and medical differences
- These microbiota-depression associations did NOT differ between ethnic groups -- suggesting the relationship is a biological invariant rather than culturally/dietarily specific
- Beta-diversity explained 29%-18% of ethnic differences in depressive symptoms
- Christensenellaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Ruminococcaceae families were prominently associated with depressive symptoms
- Largest multi-ethnic microbiome-depression study to date

**Bacteria involved:** Christensenellaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae. Direction: microbial diversity inversely associated with depression severity.

**Why it fills an evidence gap:** This is the single best paper for addressing the geographic/ethnic diversity gap (#3). While conducted in Amsterdam, the HELIUS cohort specifically recruited residents from six distinct ethnic backgrounds including Ghanaian, African Surinamese, South-Asian Surinamese, Turkish, and Moroccan populations. The finding that microbiota-depression associations are *invariant* across ethnic groups is a powerful result -- it suggests our existing Western-derived findings may generalize more broadly than feared. The N=3,211 sample also makes it one of the largest microbiome-depression studies in existence. Additionally serves the large-sample/population-based design gap (#4).

**Source:** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36473853/) | [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-34504-1)

### Paper 7: Radjabzadeh et al. 2022 -- Gut Microbiome-Wide Association Study of Depressive Symptoms

**Full citation:** Radjabzadeh D, Bosch JA, Uitterlinden AG, Zwinderman AH, Ikram MA, van Meurs JBJ, Luber AD, Scholte F, Dhber A, Bonder MJ, Haasbroek A, Collij V, Weersma RK, Amin N, Ikram MK, Lok A, Kraaij R. **Study type:** Population-based cohort (cross-sectional analysis within prospective Rotterdam Study) with independent replication

**Sample size:** Discovery cohort: 1,054 (Rotterdam Study). Replication cohort: 1,539 (Amsterdam HELIUS). Total: 2,593.

**Country/geography:** Netherlands (Rotterdam + Amsterdam)

**Key findings:**
- Identified 13 microbial taxa associated with depressive symptoms, replicated across two independent cohorts
- Associated genera: Eggerthella, Subdoligranulum, Coprococcus, Sellimonas, Lachnoclostridium, Hungatella, Ruminococcaceae (UCG002, UCG003, UCG005), Lachnospiraceae UCG001, Eubacterium ventriosum, Ruminococcus gauvreauii group

**Why it fills an evidence gap:** This is the largest and most rigorously replicated microbiome-depression association study available. The discovery + independent replication design addresses the methodological rigor gap. Published simultaneously with Bos 2022 (Paper 6 above), these two companion papers together represent the state of the art. The Rotterdam Study is a prospective population-based cohort, giving this study stronger epidemiological foundations than most of our existing cross-sectional papers. Fills the large-sample/population-based design gap (#4) and the replication gap.

**Full citation:** Pinto-Sanchez MI, Hall GB, Ghajar K, Nardelli A, Bolino C, Lau JT, Martin FP, Cominetti O, Welsh C, Rieder A, Traynor J, Gregory C, De Palma G, Pigrau M, Ford AC, Macri J, Berber B, Bergonzelli G, Surette MG, Collins SM, Moayyedi P, Bercik P. **Country/geography:** Canada (McMaster University, Hamilton, Ontario)

### Paper 9: Jacka et al. 2017 -- The SMILES Trial (Diet Intervention RCT for Depression)

**Full citation:** Jacka FN, O'Neil A, Opie R, Itsiopoulos C, Cotton S, Mohebbi M, Castle D, Dash S, Mihalopoulos C, Chatterton ML, Brazionis L, Dean OM, Hodge AM, Berk M. (2017). **Country/geography:** Australia (Deakin University, Geelong, Victoria + St Vincent's Hospital, Melbourne)

It is included because (a) the evidence gap specifically requested diet intervention RCTs, (b) the dietary intervention used is well-established to alter gut microbiota composition, and (c) it represents the foundational evidence for the diet -> mood pathway that many gut-brain axis researchers cite.

## Invariant Findings

Across all 10 papers and the broader search, these findings remained consistent:

1. **SCFA-producing bacteria are consistently depleted in disease states.** Whether Parkinson's (Keshavarzian, Unger, Sampson), depression (Radjabzadeh, Zheng), or ADHD (implicitly through diversity measures), butyrate/propionate/acetate-producing bacteria like Faecalibacterium, Coprococcus, Roseburia, and Subdoligranulum are reduced in cases vs. controls.

2. **Microbiota-mental health associations generalize across ethnic groups.** The Bos 2022 HELIUS study's finding that associations are invariant across 6 ethnic groups is critical -- it suggests the core biology is not merely a Western-diet artifact.

3. **Fecal transplant from affected humans into germ-free mice reproduces behavioral phenotypes.** This holds across PD (Sampson), depression (Zheng), and ADHD (Tengeler), establishing a consistent pattern of causal evidence.

4. **The gut-brain axis operates through multiple parallel pathways.** SCFA production, neurotransmitter precursor synthesis (dopamine in ADHD, serotonin/GABA in depression), vagal signaling (Pinto-Sanchez), neuroinflammation (Sampson), and metabolic pathways (Zheng) are all implicated. No single mechanism explains everything.

## Points of Divergence

1. **SCFAs: protective or pathogenic in Parkinson's?** Unger 2016 and Keshavarzian 2015 show reduced SCFAs in PD patients (suggesting SCFAs are protective). But Sampson 2016 found that SCFAs *promoted* neuroinflammation in germ-free ASO mice. This apparent contradiction may reflect dose-dependency, context-dependency (peripheral vs. central effects), or the difference between chronic low-level SCFA production by commensals vs. acute experimental SCFA administration. This is an active area of controversy in the field.

2. **Anxiety vs. depression respond differently to probiotics.** Pinto-Sanchez 2017 found B. longum NCC3001 improved depression but NOT anxiety. This dissociation is not universally observed (other probiotic studies show anxiety effects), suggesting strain-specificity and/or different underlying mechanisms for anxiety vs. depression.

3. **Microbiome composition vs. function.** Pinto-Sanchez 2017 found clinical improvement WITHOUT changes in fecal microbiota composition, suggesting the probiotic may act through direct neural signaling (vagus nerve) rather than reshaping the resident microbiome. This challenges the assumption that microbiome composition changes are necessary for gut-brain effects.

4. **ADHD microbiome findings are inconsistent across studies.** While Aarts 2017 found increased Bifidobacterium in ADHD, other studies (not included here) found no difference or the opposite direction. The field is still immature, and sample sizes are small.

## Rabbit Holes Explored

**The Parkinson's prodromal GI symptom timeline:** One thing that emerged from the PD papers that is *not* fully captured in our graph: Parkinson's patients develop constipation and other GI symptoms up to 20 years before motor symptoms appear. This means the gut dysbiosis is not a consequence of PD -- it precedes it. Braak's hypothesis (alpha-synuclein pathology starting in the gut and spreading to the brain via the vagus nerve) is supported by the Sampson 2016 fecal transplant evidence. Truncal vagotomy studies show reduced PD risk. The PD-microbiome story may be the strongest evidence anywhere for gut-to-brain causality.

**The SMILES trial controversy:** The Jacka 2017 SMILES trial has been criticized for small sample size (n=67), high dropout, and the impossibility of blinding a dietary intervention. A 2018 commentary in BMC Medicine ("The SMILES trial: an important first step") acknowledged these limitations while arguing the effect size was too large to dismiss. Subsequent replication attempts (HELFIMED trial, MooDFOOD trial) have produced mixed results, with some showing effects and others not. The paper remains highly cited and influential but should not be treated as definitive.

**The Radjabzadeh/Bos companion papers:** These two 2022 Nature Communications papers were published on the same day and share overlapping author teams. Radjabzadeh used the Rotterdam Study with HELIUS as replication; Bos used HELIUS as the primary cohort. They tell complementary stories: Radjabzadeh identifies specific taxa, Bos shows ethnic invariance. Reading them together gives a much richer picture than either alone.

## Edge Cases and Gotchas

1. **Zheng 2016 duplication risk.** As noted above, the existing graph lists "Zheng 2016" which is very likely this same paper. Verify before adding.

2. **Jacka 2017 does not measure the microbiome directly.** Including it in a "gut microbiome and mental health" graph requires an inferential step. The dietary intervention plausibly acts through the microbiome, but this is not demonstrated in the paper itself.

3. **Aarts 2017 sample size is very small** (n=19 ADHD). The findings are suggestive but underpowered. The predicted gene function analysis (cyclohexadienyl dehydratase) is based on 16S prediction rather than metagenomic measurement.

4. **Tengeler 2020 used pooled fecal samples** from only 3 ADHD and 3 control donors. Individual variation in microbiome composition is enormous, so pooling from 3 donors may not capture representative ADHD microbiota.

5. **Sampson 2016 used alpha-synuclein-overexpressing mice**, which are a model of genetic susceptibility to PD, not sporadic PD. The findings may not fully generalize to the ~90% of PD cases that are idiopathic.

6. **Bos 2022 HELIUS is cross-sectional.** While it addresses the ethnic diversity gap, it does not address the longitudinal design gap. The ethnic groups share a common urban environment (Amsterdam), so environmental confounding is partially controlled but dietary patterns still differ.

## Practical Implications

For the knowledge graph, these 10 papers:

1. **Triple the Parkinson's representation** (from 1 to 4 papers), with a complete mechanistic chain: taxonomic changes (Keshavarzian) -> SCFA depletion (Unger) -> causal pathology (Sampson)
2. **Establish ADHD presence** (from 0 to 2 papers), covering both association (Aarts) and causality (Tengeler)
3. **Add Ghanaian, Turkish, Moroccan, and South-Asian representation** via the HELIUS cohort (Bos 2022)
4. **Add the largest replicated microbiome-depression association study** (Radjabzadeh 2022, N=2,593)
5. **Add a specific named probiotic strain trial with fMRI** (Pinto-Sanchez 2017, B. longum NCC3001)
6. **Add the foundational diet-depression RCT** (Jacka 2017, SMILES trial)
7. **Add Chinese cohort representation** (Zheng 2016, if not already present)

## Open Questions

1. **Is the existing "Zheng 2016" in the graph this same Molecular Psychiatry paper?** If so, we need a replacement. Candidates: (a) Chen JJ et al. 2018 on sex differences in gut microbiota in MDD patients from China, or (b) a Japanese/Korean MDD-microbiome study to fill the East Asian gap specifically.

2. **Should we add a pure longitudinal/prospective paper?** The Radjabzadeh 2022 study uses data from the prospective Rotterdam Study, but the microbiome analysis itself is cross-sectional. The GUSTO study (Querdasi et al. 2025, Singapore) is truly longitudinal (stool at age 2, brain imaging at age 6, behavioral outcomes at age 7.5) but has only N=55 and is very new. The longitudinal design gap remains the hardest to fill with high-quality landmark papers, because truly longitudinal microbiome-mental health studies are rare.

3. **Should we include a Parkinson's meta-analysis** (e.g., Romano et al. 2021, npj Parkinson's Disease) to synthesize across PD studies, or are individual studies more valuable for the graph?

4. **How should Jacka 2017 be connected in the graph** given it does not directly measure microbiome? It could be linked to Ghosh 2020 (Mediterranean diet and microbiome diversity in elderly) to form a diet -> microbiome -> mood pathway.

5. **The SCFA paradox in Parkinson's** (reduced in patients but experimentally pro-inflammatory in mice) warrants deeper investigation. This may be the most important unresolved mechanistic question in the PD-microbiome field.
