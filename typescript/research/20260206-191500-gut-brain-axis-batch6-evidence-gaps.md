## Executive Summary

This batch of 10 papers was selected to systematically close the evidence gaps flagged by the researchFrontier query after 42 papers were already indexed. The most critical gaps were: (1) bipolar disorder had zero causal evidence -- we now add both an FMT-to-mice study establishing causal directionality and the largest case-control study to date; (2) PTSD had only one paper -- we add the landmark Hemmings 2017 study from South Africa, which simultaneously addresses the Africa representation gap; (3) ASD was geographically locked to Ecuador -- we add papers from Slovakia (Europe) and China (Asia); (4) diet-driven microbiome modulation gets a psychobiotic diet RCT that actually measured both microbiome composition and stress outcomes; (5) the early life developmental window gets the CHILD cohort longitudinal study (n=405) and an infant brain-imaging + microbiome study; and (6) longitudinal/prospective designs are strengthened through several of these selections.

The batch brings the total to 52 papers and addresses 7 of 7 identified gaps. Notably, the Tang et al. 2025 FMT study is the first paper in the graph providing *causal* evidence for bipolar disorder's gut-brain connection, a gap that was previously the single highest-priority deficit.

## The Question Beneath the Question

The researchFrontier analysis revealed something deeper than "we need more papers." It revealed *structural holes* in the evidence graph -- certain disorder-mechanism-geography combinations that had zero coverage, meaning any claim about those intersections was inference from adjacent nodes rather than direct evidence. The real task was not just adding 10 papers but selecting papers that maximally reduce the graph's epistemic uncertainty. Each paper was chosen to fill a specific cell in the disorder x evidence-type x geography matrix, not just to increase count.

### Paper 43: Coello et al. 2019 -- Bipolar Disorder Gut Microbiome (Denmark)

**Full Citation:** Coello K, Hansen TH, Sorensen N, Munkholm K, Kessing LV, Pedersen O, Vinberg M. "Gut microbiota composition in patients with newly diagnosed bipolar disorder and their unaffected first-degree relatives." *Brain, Behavior, and Immunity*. 2019;75:112-118. DOI: 10.1016/j.bbi.2018.09.026

**Study Type:** Case-control (cross-sectional)

**Sample Size:** 113 patients with newly diagnosed BD, 39 unaffected first-degree relatives, 77 healthy controls (N=229 total)

**Population/Geography:** Denmark (Copenhagen Affective Disorders Research Centre, Rigshospitalet)

**Key Findings:**
- Gut microbiota community membership in BD patients differed significantly from healthy controls (R2=1.0%, P=0.008)
- *Flavonifractor* (a genus linked to oxidative stress) present in 61% of BD patients vs. 39% of healthy controls; OR=2.9 (95% CI: 1.6-5.2, P=5.8x10-4)
- Unaffected first-degree relatives did not differ from controls, suggesting microbiome changes are state-related rather than familial/genetic
- Association with *Flavonifractor* weakened after adjusting for smoking in non-smoker subsample (Q=0.6), flagging smoking as a confounder

**Why It Fills a Gap:** Largest case-control study of bipolar disorder gut microbiome to date. The inclusion of unaffected relatives is a methodological strength that helps disentangle state vs. trait effects. Fills the "bipolar disorder -- no causal evidence" gap partially by providing the strongest observational foundation.

**Bacteria Mentioned:** Flavonifractor (enriched in BD)

### Paper 44: Tang et al. 2025 -- Bipolar Disorder FMT to Mice (China)

**Full Citation:** Tang A*, Jiang H*, et al. "Gut microbiota links to cognitive impairment in bipolar disorder via modulating synaptic plasticity." *BMC Medicine*. 2025;23(1):470. DOI: 10.1186/s12916-025-04313-6

**Study Type:** Translational (human case-control + FMT animal model)

**Sample Size:** Human: BD patients with cognitive impairment (BD-CI), BD without cognitive impairment (BD-nCI), and healthy controls; Mice: N=12-16 per behavioral group, N=4-7 for neurobiological analyses

**Population/Geography:** China (Zhejiang University School of Medicine, First Affiliated Hospital)

**Key Findings:**
- FMT from BD-CI patients into antibiotic-treated mice induced depression-like behavior, impaired working memory, and impaired object recognition memory
- BD-CI recipient mice showed reduced dendritic complexity and decreased PSD-95 protein expression (synaptic plasticity marker)
- Supplementation with healthy donor microbiota *partially reversed* both behavioral and neuroplasticity deficits
- *Prevotella*, *Faecalibacterium*, and *Roseburia* correlated with cognitive impairment scores

**Why It Fills a Gap:** THIS IS THE CRITICAL ADDITION. The researchFrontier flagged bipolar disorder as having 3 papers but zero causal evidence (no FMT, RCT, or animal model). This paper provides the first causal evidence in the graph via bidirectional FMT (BD-to-mice induces symptoms; healthy-to-BD-mice rescues them). The synaptic plasticity mechanism is also novel to the graph.

**Bacteria Mentioned:** Prevotella, Faecalibacterium, Roseburia (correlated with cognitive impairment)

### Paper 45: Hemmings et al. 2017 -- PTSD Gut Microbiome (South Africa)

**Full Citation:** Hemmings SMJ, Malan-Muller S, van den Heuvel LL, Demmitt BA, Stanislawski MA, Smith DG, et al. "The microbiome in posttraumatic stress disorder and trauma-exposed controls: an exploratory study." *Psychosomatic Medicine*. 2017;79(8):936-946. DOI: 10.1097/PSY.0000000000000512

**Study Type:** Case-control (exploratory)

**Sample Size:** 18 individuals with PTSD, 12 trauma-exposed controls (N=30)

**Population/Geography:** South Africa (Stellenbosch University, Western Cape)

**Key Findings:**
- No differences in alpha or beta diversity between PTSD and trauma-exposed controls
- Random forest analysis identified three phyla distinguishing PTSD status: Actinobacteria, Lentisphaerae, and Verrucomicrobia
- Decreased total abundance of these three phyla associated with higher PTSD severity (CAPS scores; r=-0.387, P=.035)
- Critical design feature: compared PTSD to *trauma-exposed* controls (not non-trauma controls), isolating the microbiome signature of the disorder from the trauma exposure itself

**Why It Fills a Gap:** Fills TWO gaps simultaneously: (1) PTSD had only 1 paper in the graph -- this is the landmark first human PTSD-microbiome study; (2) Africa-based cohorts were underrepresented -- this is from a South African population. The trauma-exposed control design is methodologically important because it separates microbiome signatures of PTSD from signatures of trauma exposure generally.

**Bacteria Mentioned:** Actinobacteria, Lentisphaerae, Verrucomicrobia (all depleted in PTSD)

### Paper 46: Tomova et al. 2015 -- ASD Gut Microbiota (Slovakia/Europe)

**Full Citation:** Tomova A, Husarova V, Lakatosova S, Bakos J, Vlkova B, Babinska K, Ostatnikova D. "Gastrointestinal microbiota in children with autism in Slovakia." *Physiology & Behavior*. 2015;138:179-187. DOI: 10.1016/j.physbeh.2014.10.033

**Study Type:** Case-control with probiotic intervention

**Sample Size:** 10 children with ASD, 9 siblings, 10 healthy controls (N=29)

**Population/Geography:** Slovakia, Europe (Comenius University, Bratislava)

**Key Findings:**
- Significant decrease in Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio in ASD children
- Elevated *Lactobacillus* spp. in ASD group
- Strong positive association between *Desulfovibrio* spp. abundance and autism severity (ADI restricted/repetitive behavior subscale)
- Probiotic supplementation normalized Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio, *Desulfovibrio* spp., and *Bifidobacterium* spp. levels
- Strong positive correlation between autism severity and GI dysfunction severity

**Why It Fills a Gap:** ASD geographic coverage was locked to Ecuador (Zurita 2020). This paper provides European representation from Slovakia. The probiotic normalization finding adds an interventional dimension. The Desulfovibrio-severity correlation is particularly relevant as Desulfovibrio has been implicated in hydrogen sulfide metabolism and gut permeability.

**Bacteria Mentioned:** Desulfovibrio (enriched, correlated with severity), Lactobacillus (enriched), Bifidobacterium (reduced, normalized by probiotic), Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio (altered)

### Paper 47: Zhang et al. 2018 -- ASD Gut Microbiota (China/Asia)

**Full Citation:** Zhang M, Ma W, Zhang J, He Y, Wang J. "Analysis of gut microbiota profiles and microbe-disease associations in children with autism spectrum disorders in China." *Scientific Reports*. 2018;8(1):13981. DOI: 10.1038/s41598-018-32219-2

**Study Type:** Case-control

**Sample Size:** 35 children with ASD, 6 typically developing controls (N=41)

**Population/Geography:** China (Asia)

**Key Findings:**
- Significant increase in Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio in ASD group (opposite direction from some Western studies)
- *Sutterella*, *Odoribacter*, and *Butyricimonas* significantly more abundant in ASD
- *Veillonella* and *Streptococcus* significantly decreased in ASD
- Butyrate and lactate producers less abundant in ASD group
- Identified microbe-disease associations using network analysis approaches

**Why It Fills a Gap:** ASD geographic diversity -- adds Asia (China) to complement the Ecuador paper and the new Slovakia paper. The reversed Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes direction compared to Western studies is itself an important finding, suggesting geography/diet may modulate which microbial shifts associate with ASD. Small control group (n=6) is a limitation worth noting.

**Bacteria Mentioned:** Sutterella, Odoribacter, Butyricimonas (enriched in ASD); Veillonella, Streptococcus (depleted in ASD)

### Paper 48: Painold et al. 2019 -- Bipolar Disorder Gut Microbiota During Depression (Austria)

**Full Citation:** Painold A, Morkl S, Kashofer K, Halwachs B, Dalkner N, Bengesser S, Birner A, Fellendorf F, Platzer M, Queissner R, Schutze G, Schwarz MJ, Moll N, Holzer P, Holl AK, Kapfhammer HP, Gorkiewicz G, Reininghaus EZ. "A step ahead: Exploring the gut microbiota in inpatients with bipolar disorder during a depressive episode." *Bipolar Disorders*. 2019;21(1):40-49. DOI: 10.1111/bdi.12682

**Study Type:** Case-control (cross-sectional)

**Sample Size:** 32 BD inpatients during depressive episode, 10 healthy controls (N=42)

**Population/Geography:** Austria (Medical University of Graz)

**Key Findings:**
- *Ruminococcaceae* and *Faecalibacterium* more abundant in healthy controls (depleted in BD)
- Phylum Actinobacteria and class Coriobacteriia more abundant in BD
- Negative correlation between microbial alpha diversity and illness duration (R=-0.408, P=0.021) -- longer illness = less diverse microbiome
- Explored correlations with inflammation, oxidative stress, tryptophan/kynurenine metabolism, and metabolic syndrome parameters
- Specifically studied BD patients *during depressive episodes*, providing state-specific data

**Why It Fills a Gap:** Complements Coello 2019 by: (a) studying BD during a specific mood state (depressive episode) rather than mixed states, (b) examining metabolic and inflammatory correlates alongside microbiome, (c) finding a dose-response-like relationship between illness duration and microbial diversity. Together, Papers 43, 44, and 48 transform bipolar disorder from a gap to a well-characterized node in the graph.

**Bacteria Mentioned:** Faecalibacterium (depleted in BD), Ruminococcaceae (depleted in BD), Actinobacteria, Coriobacteriia (enriched in BD)

### Paper 49: Berding et al. 2023 -- Psychobiotic Diet, Microbiome & Stress (Ireland)

**Full Citation:** Berding K, Bastiaanssen TFS, Moloney GM, Boscaini S, Strain CR, Anesi A, Long-Smith C, Mattivi F, Stanton C, Clarke G, Dinan TG, Cryan JF. "Feed your microbes to deal with stress: a psychobiotic diet impacts microbial stability and perceived stress in a healthy adult population." *Molecular Psychiatry*. 2023;28(2):601-610. DOI: 10.1038/s41380-022-01817-y

**Study Type:** RCT (randomized controlled trial)

**Sample Size:** 45 adults (24 psychobiotic diet, 21 control diet), 4-week intervention

**Population/Geography:** Ireland (APC Microbiome Ireland, University College Cork)

**Key Findings:**
- Psychobiotic diet (high in prebiotic and fermented foods) reduced perceived stress by 32% vs. 17% in controls (dose-dependent: higher adherence = greater reduction)
- Subtle changes in microbial composition but significant changes in 40 specific fecal lipids and urinary tryptophan metabolites
- Greater microbial *stability* (not just composition) associated with greater stress reduction
- Diet included specific targets: 6-8 servings fruits/vegetables daily, 5-8 servings grains daily, 2-3 servings fermented foods daily, and 3-4 servings legumes weekly

**Why It Fills a Gap:** This is the ideal diet-microbiome-mental health paper because it simultaneously measured (a) dietary intervention, (b) microbiome composition/stability, and (c) mental health outcomes. The "microbial stability" finding is novel -- it suggests the *consistency* of the microbiome may matter as much as its composition. From the Cryan lab (one of the most cited groups in the field), adding methodological credibility. Fills the "diet-driven microbiome modulation" gap (score 81, only 3 papers).

**Bacteria Mentioned:** Microbial stability as a whole-community metric; tryptophan metabolites as mechanism

### Paper 50: Tamana et al. 2021 -- Infant Gut Microbiome & Neurodevelopment (Canada)

**Full Citation:** Tamana SK, Tun HM, Konya T, Chari RS, Field CJ, Guttman DS, Becker AB, Moraes TJ, Turvey SE, Subbarao P, Sears MR, Pei J, Scott JA, Mandhane PJ, Kozyrskyj AL. "Bacteroides-dominant gut microbiome of late infancy is associated with enhanced neurodevelopment." *Gut Microbes*. 2021;13(1):1-17. DOI: 10.1080/19490976.2021.1930875

**Study Type:** Prospective longitudinal cohort

**Sample Size:** 405 infants (199 female) from the CHILD Cohort Study

**Population/Geography:** Canada (multi-site: Edmonton, Vancouver, Winnipeg, Toronto)

**Key Findings:**
- Three microbiome clusters identified at 12 months: Proteobacteria-dominant (22.4%), Firmicutes-dominant (46.0%), Bacteroidetes-dominant (31.6%)
- Bacteroidetes-dominant cluster associated with higher cognitive (+4.8 points), language (+4.2 points), and motor (+3.1 points) development scores at age 2
- Effect predominantly in males, not females (sex-specific effect)
- Prospective design: microbiome measured at 4 and 12 months, outcomes at 1 and 2 years
- No significant associations at the 4-month timepoint, suggesting the 12-month window is critical

**Why It Fills a Gap:** Fills the "early life window" gap directly -- this is the largest prospective study linking infant gut microbiome composition to neurodevelopmental outcomes during the critical colonization window. The CHILD cohort is one of the gold-standard birth cohorts. The sex-specific finding (males only) is an underappreciated nuance. Also fills the "longitudinal/prospective designs" gap.

**Bacteria Mentioned:** Bacteroides/Bacteroidetes (associated with better neurodevelopment), Proteobacteria (associated with poorer outcomes)

### Paper 51: Gao et al. 2019 -- Infant Gut Microbiome & Brain Functional Connectivity (USA)

**Full Citation:** Gao W, Salzwedel AP, Carlson AL, Xia K, Azcarate-Peril MA, Styner MA, Thompson AL, Geng X, Goldman BD, Gilmore JH, Knickmeyer RC. "Gut microbiome and brain functional connectivity in infants-a preliminary study focusing on the amygdala." *Psychopharmacology*. 2019;236(5):1641-1651. DOI: 10.1007/s00213-018-5161-8

**Study Type:** Cross-sectional (pilot)

**Sample Size:** 39 infants with usable fMRI data (from 89 with microbiome data), age 1 year

**Population/Geography:** USA (University of North Carolina)

**Key Findings:**
- Gut microbial alpha diversity significantly associated with amygdala-thalamus functional connectivity
- Alpha diversity also associated with anterior cingulate cortex to anterior insula connectivity (regions critical for threat processing)
- Supplementary motor area to inferior parietal lobule connectivity related to cognitive outcomes at age 2
- First study to demonstrate a direct link between infant gut microbiome diversity and brain functional connectivity patterns measured by fMRI
- Establishes the amygdala as a key node in the gut-brain axis during infancy

**Why It Fills a Gap:** Fills the "early life window" gap with a mechanistic dimension -- not just correlating microbiome with behavioral outcomes, but showing the *neural circuit* intermediary (amygdala connectivity). The amygdala focus connects to anxiety and fear processing literatures, bridging developmental and psychiatric domains. Complements Tamana 2021 (behavioral outcomes) with neural-level evidence.

**Bacteria Mentioned:** Alpha diversity as primary metric; not genus-specific

### Paper 52: Freijy et al. 2023 -- "Gut Feelings" Prebiotic Diet & Mental Health RCT (Australia)

**Full Citation:** Freijy TM, Cribb L, Oliver G, Metri NJ, Opie RS, Jacka FN, Hawrelak JA, Rucklidge JJ, Ng CH, Sarris J. "Effects of a high-prebiotic diet versus probiotic supplements versus synbiotics on adult mental health: The 'Gut Feelings' randomised controlled trial." *Frontiers in Neuroscience*. 2023;16:1097278. DOI: 10.3389/fnins.2022.1097278

**Study Type:** RCT (2x2 factorial design)

**Sample Size:** 119 adults with moderate psychological distress and low prebiotic intake, 8-week intervention

**Population/Geography:** Australia (University of Melbourne)

**Key Findings:**
- High-prebiotic diet reduced total mood disturbance vs. placebo (Cohen's d=-0.60, P=0.039)
- Probiotic supplements alone showed NO benefit (d=-0.19, P=0.51)
- Synbiotic (diet + probiotic) showed NO benefit (d=-0.03, P=0.92)
- Prebiotic diet specifically improved anxiety, stress, and sleep
- First RCT to directly compare prebiotic diet vs. probiotic supplements vs. synbiotics for mental health
- The finding that diet outperformed supplements is clinically and mechanistically significant

**Why It Fills a Gap:** Fills the "diet-driven microbiome modulation" gap with the most rigorous design -- a factorial RCT that pitted diet against supplements. The striking finding that whole-food prebiotic diet worked but isolated probiotic supplements did not challenges the supplement-focused approach that dominates the field. The 2x2 design means this single paper provides evidence for four comparisons.

**Bacteria Mentioned:** Prebiotic-responsive taxa (general); diet composition rather than specific bacteria was the focus

## Invariant Findings

Across all 10 papers and the broader 52-paper corpus, several findings remain stable:

1. **Reduced Faecalibacterium in psychiatric disorders.** Appears depleted in BD (Painold 2019), and correlated with cognitive impairment in BD (Tang 2025). Consistent with its known anti-inflammatory, butyrate-producing role.

2. **Early life is a genuine critical window.** Both Tamana 2021 (n=405) and Gao 2019 (n=39 fMRI) confirm that 12-month microbiome composition predicts later neurodevelopmental outcomes. The 4-month timepoint did NOT predict outcomes (Tamana), suggesting the window opens after initial colonization stabilizes.

3. **Sex-specific effects are real.** Tamana 2021 found Bacteroides-neurodevelopment association only in males. This echoes Aatsinki 2019 (already in graph) and Carlson 2018 (already in graph). Any meta-analysis that ignores sex is likely to underestimate true effect sizes.

4. **Diet outperforms isolated supplementation.** Both Berding 2023 (psychobiotic diet > control) and Freijy 2023 (prebiotic diet > probiotic supplement) converge on whole-food dietary patterns being more effective than single-strain or supplement interventions.

5. **The PTSD-microbiome signal involves phyla-level depletion.** Hemmings 2017 found Actinobacteria, Lentisphaerae, and Verrucomicrobia depleted in PTSD. This is a broader taxonomic pattern than the genus-level shifts seen in depression/bipolar, potentially reflecting a more severe systemic dysbiosis.

## Points of Divergence

1. **Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio in ASD.** Tomova 2015 (Slovakia) found *decreased* Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio in ASD; Zhang 2018 (China) found *increased* ratio. This is likely geography/diet-dependent -- the baseline microbiome composition differs between Central European and Chinese populations, so the direction of ASD-associated shift varies. Neither finding is "wrong" -- they reflect different starting conditions.

2. **Microbial diversity and mental health.** Painold 2019 found lower diversity associated with longer BD illness (bad). But Gao 2019 found higher alpha diversity in infants associated with *worse* brain connectivity patterns. In early life, diversity may indicate instability rather than health -- the mature-microbiome-is-good heuristic does not apply to infants.

3. **Small sample sizes vs. landmark status.** Hemmings 2017 (n=30) and Tomova 2015 (n=29) are highly cited despite small samples. They are landmark studies because they were *first* in their specific niches (PTSD-microbiome; European ASD-microbiome with probiotic intervention). Their findings need replication but remain the best available evidence for those specific intersections.

## Rabbit Holes Explored

### The Smoking Confounder in Bipolar Microbiome Research
Coello 2019 found that the Flavonifractor-BD association disappeared after adjusting for smoking in non-smokers. Smoking rates are elevated in BD populations (up to 50-70%). This means many "BD-associated" microbiome findings may actually be smoking-associated findings. Future studies must control for this, and the graph should track smoking adjustment as a quality metric.

### Microbial Stability vs. Composition
Berding 2023 found that microbial *stability* (consistency over time) predicted stress reduction better than microbial *composition* (which taxa are present). This is a paradigm-level finding that most of the field has not yet absorbed. It suggests that resilience of the microbiome to perturbation may be more important than having "the right bugs."

### The Bukowski-Thall Preprint (Not Included)
A 2023 bioRxiv preprint from the Graz group (same lab as Painold 2019) showed FMT from a BD patient to mice induced anxiety-like behavior and decreased sociability. I did not include it because it remains a preprint, but the Tang 2025 paper fills the same causal-evidence gap with a peer-reviewed publication. The two studies converge from different labs (Austria vs. China), strengthening confidence in the causal claim.

## Edge Cases & Gotchas

1. **Carlson 2018 vs. Gao 2019 overlap.** These are from the same UNC research group studying overlapping infant cohorts. Carlson 2018 (already in graph) focused on microbiome-cognition; Gao 2019 focuses on microbiome-brain connectivity via fMRI. They are complementary, not redundant, but the shared cohort should be noted.

2. **Tang 2025 uses antibiotic-treated mice, not germ-free.** This is a methodological distinction from Sharon 2019 (already in graph, which used germ-free mice for ASD). Antibiotic-treated mice retain some residual microbiota, making the FMT less "clean" but arguably more translatable to human conditions.

3. **Parletta 2019 (HELFIMED) was considered but not included** because while it is a strong Mediterranean diet RCT for depression (n=152), it did not directly measure microbiome composition, making it less useful for a gut-brain axis knowledge graph that needs to link diet -> microbiome -> mental health explicitly.

4. **O'Hare 2025 (saNeuroGut) was considered for the Africa gap** but was already in the graph. Hemmings 2017 fills both the PTSD and Africa gaps from the same South African research ecosystem (Stellenbosch University), and Malan-Muller is a co-author on both, providing continuity.

## Open Questions

1. **Is the Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio in ASD genuinely geography-dependent?** Tomova (Slovakia) and Zhang (China) found opposite directions. A multi-site study with standardized methods could resolve whether this is biological or methodological.

2. **Why does microbial stability predict stress reduction better than composition?** Berding 2023 opened this door but the mechanism is unclear. Is it that a stable microbiome produces more consistent metabolite output? Or that stress itself destabilizes the microbiome, creating a feedback loop?

3. **Can the Painold finding (diversity decreases with BD illness duration) be replicated prospectively?** If confirmed, it would suggest the microbiome degrades over the course of bipolar illness, implying early intervention could preserve microbial diversity.

4. **What is the replication status of Hemmings 2017?** The three-phyla depletion in PTSD (Actinobacteria, Lentisphaerae, Verrucomicrobia) has been cited extensively but direct replication in a larger cohort is needed. The Malan-Muller 2022 follow-up from the same group found somewhat different taxa (Catenibacterium, Mitsuokella, Olsenella, Odoribacter positively associated with PTSD symptoms), raising questions about consistency.

5. **Does the sex-specific early-life effect (males > females) in Tamana 2021 hold for psychiatric outcomes?** The neurodevelopmental effect was male-specific. Does this translate to differential psychiatric risk later in life? This connects to the broader question of sex differences in gut-brain axis development.
